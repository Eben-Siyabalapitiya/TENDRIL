One string.
Any size.

Tendril is a printed arm that curls up when you pull a string. It wraps tight at the tip and wide at the base at the same time, so the same arm can pick up something small like a screw or something fat like a roll of tape. Nothing gets adjusted in between.

Status: second prototype printed, picking things up by hand

Any size, same arm

The segments get smaller as they go, so when it curls the tip makes a tight little loop while the base makes a wide one. Whatever you hand it just ends up held by the part of the arm that fits.

One string, nothing else

No motors, no sensors, no electronics at all right now. One line runs the length of the arm, and pulling it closes every joint at once. It wraps around the shape of the object instead of having to know what the object is.

Printed in one piece

There is nothing to assemble. It comes off the printer flat and bends at the thin bits between the segments, and those same thin bits are what spring it back open when you let go of the string.

The idea

Why a spiral and not a claw

A normal gripper is only good at one size. Jaws big enough for a bottle will crush a marble, and jaws sized for a marble cannot reach around a bottle. You end up building a different gripper for every job.

A spiral gets around that, because a spiral does not really have a size. It is tight in the middle and keeps opening out as it goes, and the shape is the same the whole way, just bigger. So when the arm curls into one, it is making a small loop and a big loop at once, and the object just ends up in whichever part of it fits.

The way it gets that shape is simple. Each segment is a copy of the one before it, a little smaller every time, and there is a V-shaped gap cut between each pair. When you pull, all those gaps close together and the arm settles into the spiral on its own.

The string runs through a channel inside the arm, closer to the inner edge than the outer one. That is the whole trick. Because it sits off to one side, pulling it folds the joints shut instead of just squeezing the arm lengthwise, and the tip closes first because there is less plastic up there to fight it.

Build log

What it actually took

On paper this looks like it is just a shape. Most of the actual work has been the stuff the shape does not tell you.

  1. Two ways of drawing it

    The first thing I tried was writing a script that builds the arm for you, since the moment you change the curl even a little, every segment changes with it. That worked and it was a good way to understand the shape, but the version that actually got printed is modelled in Onshape. The script stayed as a test and a way to check my numbers.

  2. Getting a hole down the middle

    The string has to run through the inside of the arm, and it has to sit closer to one edge than the other or the thing will not fold. Lining that channel up through every segment, while each segment is a different size, took a few goes in CAD before it came out straight.

  3. The first one snapped at the base

    The first print curled fine for a few pulls and then snapped at the very bottom. Makes sense looking back. The bottom joint is holding the whole arm, so everything you pull ends up loaded onto that one thin piece of plastic, and it had a sharp corner in it, which is exactly where a crack likes to start.

  4. Fixing it with a bigger fillet

    Looked up how other people deal with this and the answer was the same everywhere: never leave a sharp inside corner where the load is. So I rounded out the bottom of every gap instead of letting them come to a point. Same arm, same curl, the stress just has somewhere to spread instead of all landing on one line. The second print has held up.

  5. Rings on the string

    Pulling a bare string with your fingers is awkward and you lose track of how far you have gone. So I tied a ring on each end, one for closing and one for opening. A finger in each ring and you can work the arm properly, which made testing way quicker than wiring up a motor every time I changed something.

  6. Putting it on a motor

    Next up. A servo with a little spool on it should be able to pull the string about as far as my fingers do. The thing I am not sure about is whether the arm springs itself back open properly once a motor is holding the line, or whether it needs an elastic cord running down the middle to help it straighten.

Where this is going

Three phases

Tendril runs through all three. This page is phase one, and the other two get added here as they happen.

  1. Now

    Build

    Rebuild something that already exists and find out what gets left out when people write it up. Print it, break it, fix it, get it picking things up.

  2. Next

    Iterate

    Take the version that works and push on it. Change things, test what actually holds, and get it better than where it started.

  3. After

    Create

    Use what the first two taught me to build something that is mine.
